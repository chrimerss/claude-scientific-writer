## 1. Earth Science to Action (ES2A) Strategy & Alignment

### ES2A Vision and Mission
- **Vision**: A thriving world, driven by trusted, actionable Earth science.
- **Mission**: Innovate and collaborate to explore and understand the Earth system, make new discoveries, and enable solutions for the benefit of all.
- **Goal**: Within a decade, advance and integrate Earth science knowledge to empower humanity to create a more resilient world.
- **Objectives**: (1) Holistically observe, monitor, and understand the Earth system. (2) Deliver trusted information to drive Earth resilience activities.

### The ES2A Pyramid

The pyramid illustrates how foundational research flows upward into societal impact through a **virtuous cycle** in which user needs feed back into future programs and missions:

1. **Foundational Knowledge, Technology, Missions & Data** — sensor innovation, satellite missions, observations from space/air/ground
2. **Earth System Science & Applied Research** — predictive modeling, science applications, climate change mitigation/adaptation/response
3. **Solutions & Societal Value** — open-source science, climate services, tools and applications to inform decisions
4. **Public Understanding & Exchange** — scientific understanding in public sphere, applied science to users, multi-way information exchange

**Virtuous Cycle**: User needs inform the next iteration of programs, missions, and initiatives → better foundational knowledge → better applications → wider use → richer user feedback.

### Three Earth Action Principles

1. **Scaling** — Maximize impact by extending successful applications to new users; build on existing research innovations, not start from scratch.
2. **Building Bridges** — Connect NASA research outcomes to public/private sector organizations. Research → practical applications → new research needs (virtuous cycle).
3. **User-centered Science** — Put user needs at the center; engage users as full partners in development and implementation.

### The Earth Science to Action Continuum

```
SCIENTIFIC INNOVATIONS → USER NEEDS → APPLIED SOLUTIONS → SOCIETAL BENEFIT
```

**Proposal implication**: Reviewers evaluate whether the proposal maps onto this continuum end-to-end. A proposal that stops at "applied solutions" without demonstrating "societal benefit" (via a specific end user's improved decision-making) is incomplete.

## 2. User Engagement Framework — Five Tasks

NASA expects proposals to demonstrate genuine user engagement. The following five-task framework, while most relevant to new collaborations at the Discovery phase (ARLs 1–2), represents good practice at all stages.

### Task 1: Know the Territory

Before reaching out to users, invest time learning about them:

**Research the landscape:**
- Identify organizations whose mandates align with your expertise (federal, state, local agencies; private sector; NGOs; communities)
- Note their existing data use, partnerships, and mandates
- Use structured approaches: landscape scan, gap analysis, situational analysis

**Entry points for finding users:**
- Tap existing relationships — check if colleagues already have connections
- Do background research — reports, case studies, evaluations, journal articles, trade media
- Read what users read — sector-specific trade publications and media
- Go to their meetings — attend conferences in their field (not just AGU/AMS)
- Leverage NASA contacts — Earth Action Program Managers at HQ and NASA centers
- Join ongoing collaborations — team up with researchers already working with users
- Volunteer for review panels — learn about leading topics and meet colleagues
- Organize listening tours — targeted conversations with user community members

**Checklist — Things to Understand:**
- [ ] Actors and relationships (who, roles, responsibilities, connections)
- [ ] Current activities and resources (existing initiatives, capacities, tools, funding)
- [ ] Needs, gaps, and barriers (where current efforts fall short, constraints, risks)
- [ ] Opportunities and trends (emerging practices, innovations, policy windows)

### Task 2: Reach Out and Listen

**Key insight**: Don't start with "What do you need?" Instead, use experiential questions that surface pain points and real-world context:

- "Tell me about a day at your job. What do you spend your time on?"
- "What work problems keep you up at night?"
- "What would you like your organization to be better at?"
- "Tell me about a problem you haven't solved."
- "What would you like to be able to do that you can't do now?"
- "What's a decision or action you'd like to be more certain about?"
- "What information don't you have now that you wish you had?"

**Communication principle**: "Listen. Ask. Repeat." — successful applications scientists are practiced at this cycle.

**Building rapport**: Users want scientists who listen first, highlight opportunities (not just risks), co-develop ideas from the start, respect their experience, use plain language, and start small.

### Task 3: Frame the Challenge Together

**Needs assessment questions — organized by domain:**

**Decisions:**
- What decision(s) does the user organization make?
- What kinds of analysis support those decisions?
- What types of actions do they take based on the decisions?
- What capacity do they need to sustain improved decision-making?

**Information and Models:**
- How frequently do they need information products?
- How timely must those products be?
- What data formats and systems of units do they prefer?
- What grid spacings do their models use?
- What additional capacity might they need for sustainability?

**User Community:**
- What audiences do users serve (their own clients/customers/partners)?
- How might the application help serve those audiences better?
- What are the users' trusted sources of information?

**Structured needs assessment tools:**
| Tool | When to Use |
|---|---|
| Stakeholder Mapping / Network Analysis | Understand institutions, policies, relationships, influence |
| Key Informant Interviews & Focus Groups | In-depth qualitative insights, building trust |
| Surveys & Questionnaires | Large-group input, comparing responses, quantifying trends |
| Journey Mapping | Map user process steps, identify pain points and decision moments |
| Personas & User Profiles | Synthesize needs of multiple user groups |
| Co-Design Workshops | Engage users in testing/refining ideas, foster buy-in |

### Task 4: Co-design a Solution

Co-design goes beyond consultation — it requires active, systematic iteration with users. Topics to address:

- **Products and Outputs**: Expected outputs (datasets, models, dashboards), delivery methods, documentation, data limitations
- **Data**: Required inputs from NASA and other sources, data management, formats, interoperability, validation, open data policies
- **Roles and Responsibilities**: Partner contributions, technical responsibilities, required approvals, expected commitments
- **Sustainability**: Future feasibility, hardware/software needs, human/financial resources, sustainability tasks
- **Training and Capacity**: Current competencies, target audience, training methods, language needs, immediate and long-term needs

**Critical co-design principle** (from NASA scientists): "Don't sneak in more science than what they are hoping to fund." The emphasis is on engagement and demonstrated use that forms the basis of decision-making in someone else's environment.

### Task 5: Formalize the Proposal

Each solicitation has specific requirements. Key elements typically include:

**User Engagement Plan** — describes how team and users will interact throughout the project:
- Timeline for engagement aligned with project goals and user activities
- Methods of engagement (workshops, meetings, calls)
- Identification of risks, challenges, and barriers
- Budget resources for engagement activities

**Transition and Sustainability Plan** (especially for higher ARLs):
- Path for moving application into users' environment during project period
- Users' ability to sustain the application (human/financial/institutional resources)
- Capacity building (documentation, training, support)
- Anticipated barriers to adoption and process for overcoming them

## 3. Three Building Blocks for Proposals

NASA frames strong Earth Action proposals around three building blocks:

1. **Relationships with users are the starting point** — build relationships before a solicitation is announced; even if connections start at proposal time, meaningful collaboration throughout the lifecycle is essential
2. **Listen closely to uncover the real, specific challenges** — understand goals, obstacles, workflows, data use, technical resources, and data limitations; iterate with users to bring science to a useful point
3. **Fit the science to the problem** — don't be "a hammer in search of a nail"; collaboration should be driven by user needs, not by a desire to promote a dataset or model; "less is more" when simpler tools enable user adoption

**Proposal test**: Can you articulate in two sentences: (1) why now, (2) why this approach, and (3) who is going to use it? If yes, you're ready to write an Earth Action proposal.

## 4. Co-development Methods

### Human-Centered Design (HCD)

Example: NMOSE hydrological monitoring project (ARL 7→9 over 3 years)

**Year 1 (Design)**: Repeated consultations with water-rights managers, hydrologists, and legal staff → refined data needs (resolution, format, latency) and dashboard UX  
**Year 2 (Sustainability)**: Heavy emphasis on operability and transition — workshops, social media-style video demos, step-by-step guides, online discussion boards  
**Year 3 (Impact Assessment)**: Users co-developed structured approach to monitoring change with regular feedback

**HCD Method — Needs-Validation Storyboarding**: Share 20 cartoon vignettes showing before/after scenes of fictional characters facing familiar challenges. Instead of asking "how can the application help you," storyboards let users react honestly and identify relevant capabilities.

### Structured Decision-Making (SDM)

Example: Alaska SWOT river/glacial lake monitoring (ARL 7→9 over 3 years)

**Method**: Step-by-step process to define the decision context, clarify objectives, identify solutions, analyze trade-offs, address uncertainty, and decide. Used scenario planning in brainstorming sessions where collaborators considered ~12 application concepts and pursued 4.

**Key lesson**: "Hear what users are telling you." The team originally focused on river discharge, but users repeatedly said glacial lake monitoring was their priority. Listening and iterating changed the entire project focus.

### User Confidence Progression

Users progress through these stages — proposals should address where the user currently is and what the project will advance them to:

1. Awareness of the data
2. Realization that the data can inform their work
3. Willingness to try using the data
4. Confidence in searching for better data to answer their questions
5. Integration of the data in their decision-making processes
6. User adoption, adaptation, and maintenance of the data products
7. Future development of the data products

## 5. Impact Definition Framework

### NASA's Three-Tier Impact Model

| Tier | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Use** | Extent of application use by users and/or public | Drought tool information included in Navajo Nation's monthly bulletin sent to 80 decision-makers |
| **Action** | Real-world activities and changes in users' decisions | Authorities enact temporary shipping speed restrictions based on breeding whale movement predictions |
| **Benefit** | Positive results for users, partners, or environment | Improved tornado forecasts enable earlier warnings, resulting in dramatic mortality declines |

### Outputs, Outcomes, and Impact

| Level | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Outputs** | Products, services, capacities the project delivers | Maps produced, datasets developed, tools built, trainings held |
| **Outcomes** | Changes in user activities, decisions, performance | Improved decision-making, more effective food assistance distribution, targeted mosquito control |
| **Impact** | Higher-level social, economic, or environmental change | Lives saved, fewer food-insecure people, fewer malaria cases |

### Performance Metrics Principles
- **Right-size**: Match monitoring effort to project scale and resources
- **Start early**: Establish baseline immediately, even at low effort
- **Align with users**: Link project metrics to users' decision-making metrics
- **Align with funders**: Ensure metrics feed into NASA's M&E frameworks
- Develop metrics *with* users, not for them

### Theory of Change Framework

Used by NASA Lifelines project: a structured framework mapping how actions lead to desired outcomes across tiers:
- **IF** program objectives are supported (fostering EO use, increasing partnerships, identifying new research questions, building multidisciplinary community, increasing investment)
- **THEN** changes in humanitarian response will include: more reliable, better coordinated, faster, more flexible, more tailored, more effective, more efficient
- **AND** high-level impact: targeted humanitarian action

## 6. Team Composition for Earth Action

### Characteristics of Successful EA Teams

- **Varied skills and backgrounds** — diverse perspectives create more useful, accessible applications
- **Multiple disciplines** — beyond remote sensing: ML, cloud computing, statistics, data visualization, UX; especially for non-traditional sectors (health, insurance, transportation)
- **Personal investment** — passionate team members multiply impact
- **Good communicators** — ability to translate science to plain language; language skills for diverse communities

### User Placement on the Team
- Users should be listed as **Co-PI, Co-I, or collaborator** on the project team
- In some cases, users may be funded through the grant
- See NASA GCAM for formal role definitions

### "Last Mile" Staffing
The skills needed to transition an application differ from those that developed it. "The last mile might be a different set of skills" — IT/programming support for APIs, data services, operational hosting vs. the scientists who developed the algorithm.

## 8. Strong Proposal Checklist

### Proposal Characteristics (from NASA Earth Action)

- [ ] Compelling rationale for the application that will excite reviewers — **start with people!**
- [ ] Simple, readable, and understandable
- [ ] Conveys the problem, why it must be addressed, and how NASA Earth data will support its solution
- [ ] Maps out collaboration with a specific user or users
- [ ] Uses accessible, powerful images and figures to reinforce key messages
- [ ] Describes the project in detail with credible anticipated outcomes
- [ ] Provides definitions of success and evaluation plans
- [ ] Addresses sustainability, especially on data management and user skills/capacity
- [ ] Identifies a community of interested partners
- [ ] Presents the team (especially the PI) in a way that inspires confidence
- [ ] Includes a complete, right-sized budget

### Pro Tips (from NASA officials and funded PIs)

1. **Read the entire solicitation very carefully** — review the evaluation criteria
2. **Write with reviewers in mind** — let your passion shine through
3. **Strive for simplicity and readability**, not complexity
4. **Highlight key messages** with visual cues (boxes, bold text, different fonts)
5. **Describe impact of past work**, not just the who/what/when/how
6. **Use the proposal process to demonstrate leadership** and team collaboration
7. **Balance specificity with flexibility** — assure reviewers of responsiveness to user needs
8. **Get feedback from people outside your discipline**
9. **Engage funders as process allows** but don't overwhelm them
10. **Be prepared for specialized requirements** — communications plans, user engagement plans, M&E plans

### Strong Abstract Checklist

A strong abstract quickly and persuasively explains:
- [ ] The challenge you are trying to address
- [ ] Your solution and its expected impact
- [ ] What is unique about your solution and why it will be effective
- [ ] Why YOU are the person to lead the project
- [ ] Why YOUR TEAM is right for the project
- [ ] Why NOW is the time for this project

### Dr. Max Bernstein's Rule
> "Don't just write a proposal that can be understood, write a proposal that cannot be misunderstood."

## 9. Transition & Sustainability

### Five Sustainability Pillars

Plan for all five from Day One:

1. **Data management** — policies and systems for data throughout application lifecycle
2. **Data, software, and computational infrastructure** — hardware, software, cloud, and platforms the user needs
3. **Long-term requirements** — ongoing costs, maintenance, updates
4. **User skills and technical capacity** — training, documentation, ongoing support
5. **Enabling institutional authorities or policies** — user's organizational mandate and approval to use the application

### Transition Best Practices

| Practice | Description |
|---|---|
| Transparency and early communication | Set expectations about closure in advance |
| Shared decision-making | Clarify transition roles and responsibilities together |
| User-friendly documentation | Detailed, practical, accessible documentation for tools, datasets, methods |
| Continued relationships | Clearly define level of ongoing cooperation and support post-project |
| Reflection, learning, sharing | Formally and jointly reflect on what was learned; share lessons |
| Acknowledgment and recognition | Ensure all reports, articles, presentations recognize all partners |

### Capacity Development Checklist

- [ ] Co-develop training plan with users
- [ ] Use multiple methods (in-person workshops, online training, self-paced, peer-to-peer, short videos)
- [ ] Establish offline/asynchronous platforms for interaction
- [ ] Test materials with users and revise based on feedback
- [ ] Target various audiences with tailored learning objectives (core technical group, senior leaders, broader stakeholders)
- [ ] Localize and adapt content to users' experience and technical level
- [ ] Build skills progressively over time
- [ ] Use students/interns for one-on-one assistance during in-person training
- [ ] Plan strategies to institutionalize the learning
- [ ] Provide coaching and follow-up support
- [ ] Evaluate and improve

## 10. Implementation Challenges — Hurdle Taxonomy & Failure Scenarios

### Common Application Hurdles

**Institutional Barriers:**
- Bureaucracy — slow/multilayered decision-making
- Cost-sharing requirements — may be a stumbling block for some users
- Conflicting data environments — platform/dataset/AI restrictions
- Funding delays — resources not arriving on schedule
- Publish or perish — academic pressures competing with implementation
- Project lifecycle mismatch — application may need more time than typical 2–3 year cycles

**Partnership Pitfalls:**
- Changing circumstances — user priorities shift with leadership/staff changes
- Weak collaboration — neglected relationships, inconsistent management, poor communication
- Partner burnout — users juggling many responsibilities lose enthusiasm
- Partner expectations — disappointment if application takes longer or underdelivers
- Human capacity — staff turnover requiring repeated training
- Data management sustainability — difficulty funding post-project infrastructure (cloud, compute, skills)

### Three Real-World Failure Scenarios

**Scenario 1: Concerns Over Uncertainty (Land Use Permits)**
- Agency used parametric statistics on field data; NASA team integrated satellite data enabling much larger area coverage
- Challenge: Remote sensing uses different statistical methods + always reports error metrics → agency feared legal challenges from "added uncertainty"
- Resolution: Worked directly with the manager to explain the statistics; developed a statistically-valid framework based on remote sensing data; published in sector journals the agency reads

**Scenario 2: Unexpected Results (Grassland Monitoring)**
- Goal: Use vegetation indices + remote sensing to assess sustainability practices on grasslands
- Challenge: Bare ground was so homogenous that data lacked specificity for requested indicators
- Resolution: Found different but valuable questions the data could address; used phenological types and climate data to align remote sensing with ground data in areas with clearer signal

**Scenario 3: Data Failure (GPM Calibration → Insurance Payouts)**
- Parametric insurance company used GPM satellite rainfall data with automated threshold-based payouts
- Challenge: GPM Core Observatory had short-term calibration problem → unrealistic 900mm/day readings across multiple sites → automated payout reports generated
- Resolution: Used backup data source (NOAA CPC rain gauge analysis), reran calculations, communicated openly, built automated quality-control system to flag future anomalies, established communication protocols as standard operations

## 11. Application Readiness Levels (ARLs) — Detailed Pathway

### ARL Definitions

| ARL | Label | Description | Phase |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Basic research | Fundamental Earth science | — |
| 2 | Invention: Application concept | Application idea defined with end users | Discovery, Concept, Feasibility |
| 3 | Viability established | Proof of application concept | Discovery, Concept, Feasibility |
| 4 | Prototype/plan | Initial integration and verification | Development, Testing, Validation |
| 5 | Potential determined | Validation in relevant environment | Development, Testing, Validation |
| 6 | Potential demonstrated | Demonstration in relevant environment | Development, Testing, Validation |
| 7 | Functionally demonstrated | Application prototype in partner's decision-making | Deployment, Transition, Adoption |
| 8 | Functionally proven | Application completed and qualified | Deployment, Transition, Adoption |
| 9 | Sustained use | Approved, operational deployment and use in decision-making | Deployment, Transition, Adoption |

### EA Proposal Types by ARL

**Early Stage** (ARL 2–4):
- Develop new applications with well-proven algorithms
- Test feasibility, proof-of-concept, or create early prototypes
- Advance at least one ARL per year

**Advanced Stage** (ARL 5→8/9):
- More mature projects aiming for deployment in user's environment
- Must have defined application with end users (ARL 2), feasibility work (ARL 3), working prototype (ARL 4)
- Includes plan to transition application into sustained decision-making

### Applications Pathway — Three Concurrent Tracks

**People Track:**
1. Discovery — Build relationships, understand needs, engage others, be open to skepticism
2. Concept — Iterate with users, listen to feedback, affirm commitment, agree on collaboration
3. Development — Collaborate, prioritize user needs, communicate data opportunities and limitations
4. Deployment — Build user capacity, provide ongoing support
5. New Opportunities — Stay in touch, improve application, work with new users to scale

**Science Track:**
1. Discovery — Listen, understand needs, assess how your work fills gaps
2. Concept — Formalize concept, consider sustainability, modify and adapt
3. Development — Create prototype, iterate, ensure users can sustain, automate outputs
4. Deployment — Ensure smooth operation in user environments, troubleshoot
5. New Opportunities — Publish, pursue related research questions

**Management Track:**
1. Discovery — Define objectives, identify opportunities (funding, partnerships)
2. Concept — Develop project plan, choose PM approach, build diverse team
3. Development — Motivate team, plan training/support/infrastructure, communicate progress
4. Deployment — Support adoption, invest resources to clear hurdles, evaluate impact, communicate stories
5. New Opportunities — Identify new applications, share lessons learned

**"Fast fail" gate**: If the application is not viable after Discovery, review findings and concept before investing further.

## 12. Communications & Storytelling

### Communications Plan Framework

| Component | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Audience** | Application team, users, their stakeholders, NASA program managers, funders, general public |
| **Goals** | Inform decisions, build trust, encourage collaboration, foster buy-in and use, demonstrate value |
| **Content & Messages** | Technical findings, progress updates, lessons learned, real-world impact stories |
| **Methods** | Meetings, newsletters, workshops, social media, journal articles, trade publications, media outreach |
| **Monitoring** | Targets/metrics to assess whether communications goals are being met |

### Storytelling Arc for EA Applications

1. **Beginning (Setup)**: Introduce who is involved and what's at stake
2. **Middle (Challenge)**: Build tension — what problem needs to be solved
3. **End (Change)**: Show how an Earth Action application delivers a positive benefit

**Proposal implication**: Frame the DMA and significance sections as stories — the challenge the end user faces, what the project changes, and the societal benefit that results.
