# My Writing Style: Zhi Li

Hydrology, flood modeling, hydrometeorology, remote sensing. CU Boulder (CEAE / INSTAAR).
Target venues: Nature-family, Science-family, AGU, AMS, IOP, Elsevier.

This profile was built from ~9,400 words of first-author prose (abstracts, introductions,
discussions, and conclusions) across six published papers. The numbers below are measured,
not estimated. Reproduce them.

---

## 1. Two hard rules (author-specified)

These override anything else in this file when they conflict.

### Rule 1. Em-dash restraint

Budget: **at most 1 dash construction per 500 words.** Never more than two in a section.
Never two in the same paragraph.

The one permitted use is the **paired appositive** that defines or unpacks a term
immediately after naming it:

> We propose a development—the Flashiness-Intensity-Duration-Frequency (F-IDF) curve—to
> quantify flash flood intensity.

> The NOAA ground-based radars—WSR-88D network—provide nationwide precipitation
> observations.

Never use a dash to:

- replace a colon before a list or an explanation
- replace a comma in a normal appositive
- staple a second thought onto a finished sentence
- create emphasis or a rhetorical pause

Default replacements, in order of preference: a period, a comma pair, a colon, parentheses.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| `X is important — it drives Y.` | `X is important. It drives Y.` |
| `three factors — A, B, and C` | `three factors: A, B, and C` |
| `the model — developed at OU — is …` | `the model, developed at OU, is …` |

If a paired dash is used, both dashes must be present. A lone trailing dash is always wrong.

### Rule 2. No complex sentences

One main clause plus **at most one** subordinate clause. Prefer zero.

- Target median **20–22 words**; corpus mean is 23.6.
- **Hard cap 35 words.** Split anything longer.
- Two commas is normal; three or more is a signal to split.
- Do not chain `which` clauses. Only 6% of the author's sentences contain `which` at all.
- Do not stack a leading subordinate clause *and* a trailing one on the same sentence.
- Never use a semicolon to fuse two independent clauses (see §3).

When a sentence exceeds the cap, split at the logical seam and let the second sentence
begin with the connective:

> **Before (44 w):** Although convection-permitting models resolve convective processes and
> therefore improve sub-daily rainfall rates, their computational cost has restricted their
> use to short retrospective windows, which limits what they can say about flood dynamics at
> climate time scales.
>
> **After (2 × ~21 w):** Convection-permitting models resolve convective processes and
> improve sub-daily rainfall rates. However, their computational cost restricts them to short
> retrospective windows, limiting what they reveal about flood dynamics at climate scales.

Both rules apply "if allowed". That is, they hold unless a target journal's house style, a
co-author, or a direct quotation requires otherwise. Note the exception rather than silently
breaking the rule.

## 2. Voice in one paragraph

Declarative, quantified, and unhurried. State the hazard, size it with a number, name the
gap, then say plainly what was done. First person plural is the default subject for
methodological and interpretive moves. Claims are hedged with a small, repeated set of modals
rather than with elaborate qualification. Every section closes on a use. It names who acts on
this, and how. The prose is not decorative. It earns attention by being specific.

## 3. Measured fingerprint

| Feature | Target | Corpus value |
|---|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | 22–24 words | 23.6 |
| Median sentence length | 20–22 words | 22 |
| Sentences ≤ 20 words | ~43% | 42.5% |
| Sentences > 40 words | ≤ 5% | 4.3% |
| Sentences with no comma | ~1 in 3 | 32% |
| Subordinators per sentence | ≤ 0.6 | 0.60 |
| Dash constructions | ≤ 2 per 1,000 words | 2.2 |
| Semicolons outside `(a); (b); (c)` lists | 0 | ~0 |
| Parentheses | frequent, for numerics and acronyms | 12 per 1,000 words |
| `We` as sentence subject | dominant over agentless passive | 61 vs 55 |
| Explicit `%` figures | dense in abstract and results | 28 in 9.4k words |

Sentence openers, in the author's own frequency order:
`The` > `This` > `In …` > `We` > `However,` > `For example/instance` > `To` > `As` > `These`.

*Validation.* These targets were checked against a held-out 9,800 words (the Results section
of the Science Advances paper and sections 3–4 of the ERL review), which were not used to
build the profile. Median sentence length 22, sentences ≤20 words 47%, no-comma sentences 37%,
subordinators 0.45 per sentence, `which` in 4% of sentences, dashes 1.5 per 1,000 words. The
median is the reliable anchor; the mean drifts upward in results sections that carry many
parenthetical figure references.

## 4. Paragraph architecture

Nearly every paragraph follows one of four shapes. Use them.

**A. Claim → evidence → number → consequence.**
Topic sentence states the claim flatly. Second sentence supplies the mechanism or citation.
Third supplies a figure. Last states what it means.

**B. Prior work → limitation → our move.**
Summarize what has been done, name the specific shortcoming, then pivot with `However,` or
`In contrast,` and state the contribution.

**C. Enumerated reasoning.**
Announce the count, then march through it: `We hypothesize three reasons for this research
gap. First, … Second, … Third, …` This is the author's most distinctive habit, at 18
enumeration markers in 9.4k words. Use it for research gaps, limitations, and explanations
of why results differ from prior work.

**D. Definition paragraph.**
Name the term, define it operationally with a threshold, cite the support, then say why the
threshold is defensible. `Here, we define rice-killing events as periods when flood depth
equals or exceeds crop height, resulting in complete submergence for at least 7 days.`

**E. Goal → objectives → implication.**
Reserved for the final paragraph of the introduction. See §5, habit 4, and
`references/section-moves.md` P5.

## 5. Non-negotiable habits

1. **Open on the hazard with a superlative, then quantify it in the next sentence.**
   `Floods in the United States are the most devastating water-related natural hazards.`
   followed immediately by dollars, fatalities, and a date range.
2. **Pivot with `However,`.** It is the workhorse, with 16 uses in the corpus, far ahead of
   `Nevertheless` (1) and `Nonetheless` (0). Do not substitute fancier connectives.
3. **Announce the contribution with `Here we …` or `In this study, …`,** in the active voice,
   naming the models and the resolution.
4. **Close the introduction with goal, then objectives, then implication, in that order.**
   This is the author's most reliable structural habit. All six papers do it, and the
   introduction is not finished until all three moves are on the page.
   - **Goal.** One sentence, stated at the highest level. `The goal of this study is to …`,
     `This study hopes to provide …`, `In doing so, we quantify …`, `Hence, we introduce …
     for the first time.`
   - **Objectives.** The goal decomposed into two to four verb-led tasks. Lettered with
     semicolons is the fullest form: `The aim of this article is threefold: (a) …; (b) …;
     and (c) ….` This is the only place semicolons appear. A compact `we quantify … and
     identify …` pair is the shorter form. In review articles the objectives become the
     section roadmap. Never more than four.
   - **Implication.** The last sentence of the introduction. It names a user and a decision,
     not a finding. `The results of this study can inform stakeholders and decision-makers'
     plans to …`, `It can be served as a basis for adapting …`, `meaning that it can be
     integrated into any flood forecast system.`

   Keep the goal at a higher altitude than the objectives it decomposes into. Never stop after
   the objectives.
5. **Put the region and the percentage together.** `The Southwest (+10.5%)`,
   `China's East Coast experiencing 14% losses`, `(r = −0.5, P < 0.05)`.
6. **Write a limitations paragraph that concedes first and enumerates second.**
   Open with a flat concession (`This work by no means intends to deliver an exhaustive
   depiction of future floods.`), then `First, … Second, … Last, …`.
7. **Close on the actor.** End sections and papers by naming who uses the result:
   emergency responders, decision-makers, stakeholders, forecasters, the community.
8. **Define on first use, in place.** Use `denoted as`, `termed as`, `referred to as`,
   `heretofore`. Not a footnote, and not a later glossary.

## 6. Things this voice does not do

- Rhetorical questions, except the single framing question in an introduction
  (`How do future floods evolve under a changing climate?`), at most one per paper.
- Adjective stacking or literary metaphor.
- Long agentless passive chains. Prefer `We extract flood events` over
  `Flood events were extracted`.
- Hedging by accumulation (`it may possibly be somewhat likely that`). Pick one modal.
- Bullet lists inside manuscript prose. Enumerate inside sentences instead.
- Bare citations as sentence subjects without a claim attached.

## 7. Proofreading checks

Recurring slips worth a targeted pass before submission:

- Word-choice near-misses: `border practices` → `broader practices`; `in realty` → `in reality`;
  `amendable` → `amenable`; `emerging counties` → `emerging countries`.
- `deemphasize`, `peroids`, `devasting`. Spellcheck does not always catch these in context.
- Article drop before singular count nouns: `landslide model` → `a landslide model`.
- `It's` in formal prose → `it is`.
- Comma splices in the enumerated `First, … Second, …` runs.
